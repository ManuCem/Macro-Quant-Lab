Spanish economy · Euro boom to today

What drove Spain's economy from the euro boom to today?

Six periods, each with a different driver. As Spain joined the euro, its 3-month interest rate fell from 8.9% (1995) to 3.1% (1999), and household and company debt rose from 85% to 216% of GDP by 2007. The bust and the euro debt crisis took unemployment from 7.9% (May 2007) to 26.4% (Feb 2013). By Q3 2026 real GDP was +21% above its 2008 peak (euro area +18%), while unemployment, at 10.3%, was still above the euro area's 6.5%.

Spanish unemployment swings far more than the euro area's, in both directions. Two reasons come back in several tabs: construction and tourism are large and move with the cycle, and many jobs were on temporary contracts, which are cheap to end (see 2008–2009).

Period 1 of 6 · 1995–2007

Why did Spain grow so fast after joining the euro?

Real GDP grew +3.7% a year on average, against +2.4% in the euro area.

Joining the euro brought German-level interest rates to an economy with higher inflation. Borrowing became very cheap in real terms, credit flowed into housing, and construction became a much larger part of the economy. Spending grew faster than income, and the difference was borrowed from abroad.

1995–2007 · 01

What happened to interest rates when Spain joined the euro?

The real short-term rate averaged 0.1% in Spain in 1999–2007 (below zero in 48% of months); for the euro area as a whole the same rate meant 1.2%.

How to read it. Three lines, all in %. The 3-month interest rate is what banks charge each other for 3-month loans (Euribor from 1999, the same in the whole euro area); mortgages and business loans are priced off it. Inflation is the yearly rise in Spanish consumer prices. The real rate is the first minus the second: what borrowing costs once inflation is removed.

To join the euro, Spain had to bring inflation and interest rates down to the level of the core countries. After 1999 the ECB set one interest rate for the whole euro area, based on average euro-area inflation. Spanish inflation was higher (3.1% a year in 1999–2007 against 2.1% for the euro area), so the same rate was a lower real rate in Spain: about 1.1 points lower than for the euro area on average. A real rate near or below zero makes borrowing to buy a house or to build very attractive: this is where the credit boom starts.

In 2006 investment was 30% of GDP in Spain against 22% in the euro area. Building homes counts as output while it happens, but homes built for buyers who are no longer there add little future output. With so much activity and so many jobs in one sector, the whole economy depended on one price: the price of housing.

Three weak spots grew during the boom. Foreign borrowing: Spanish banks financed part of the credit by borrowing abroad, and that kind of funding can stop suddenly. Competitiveness: wages and prices rose faster than productivity (35% against 17% for the euro area), and inside the euro there is no currency to devalue. One sector: growth leaned on construction. Public finances looked solid: debt fell to 36% of GDP in 2007 (euro area 66%) with a surplus of 1.9% of GDP. But part of that tax revenue came from the housing boom itself.

Period 2 of 6 · 2008–2009

What happened when the bubble burst?

Real GDP fell 4.4% from Q2 2008 to Q1 2009, and unemployment rose from 7.9% to 18.9% in two years.

House prices stopped rising in 2007, and in 2008 the global financial crisis cut off the cheap funding Spanish banks relied on. Building stopped, construction workers lost their jobs, and households and firms with large debts cut their spending.

2008–2009 · 01

How fast did output fall?

Peak to bottom: Spain −4.4%, euro area −5.7%, Germany −7.0%.

How to read it. Real GDP, Q1 2008 = 100. A value of 96 means output 4% below its early-2008 level. The dashed line marks the failure of the US bank Lehman Brothers (Sep 2008), when the global crisis became acute.

Spain's fall in output was smaller than the euro area's, and Germany, an exporter, fell most when world trade collapsed. Spain's big difference was in jobs, as the next panel shows. The ECB cut its main rate from 3.75% (Oct 2008) to 1.00% (May 2009), but with banks and borrowers loaded with property debt, cheaper money did not restart lending.

2008–2009 · 02

Why did unemployment rise so much more than in the euro area?

1.3 million jobs were lost in 2007–2009, 65% of them in construction.

How to read it. Top: unemployment rate, % of the labour force. Bottom: employees on temporary (fixed-term) contracts, % of all employees, 1995 to today.

Unemployment rate, %

Temporary contracts, % of employees

Unemployment rose from 7.9% to 18.9% in Spain, against 7.4% to 10.1% in the euro area. Two features explain the gap. Construction alone accounted for 65% of the jobs lost. Temporary contracts: in 2007 32% of Spanish employees had one (16% in the euro area). Ending them is cheap, so firms adjusted by cutting staff rather than hours or wages, while in Germany many firms kept workers on reduced hours. Young workers, who held most temporary contracts, were hit first.

2008–2009 · 03

What happened to public finances?

The budget went from a surplus of 1.9% of GDP in 2007 to a deficit of 11.2% in 2009.

How to read it. Government revenue minus spending, % of GDP, each year. Above zero is a surplus, below zero a deficit.

Most of the swing happened by itself: taxes linked to property sales and construction collapsed, and spending on unemployment benefits rose with unemployment (automatic stabilisers). Public debt rose from 36% to 53% of GDP in two years, still below the euro area's 80%. But a deficit of 11.2% of GDP (euro area 6.3%) is what investors started to watch in 2010.

Period 3 of 6 · 2010–2013

Why did Spain fall into a second recession?

Real GDP shrank again in 2012 (−2.9%) and 2013 (−1.4%), and unemployment peaked at 26.4% in Feb 2013.

From 2010 doubts about Greece spread to other euro countries with large deficits or weak banks. Spain's borrowing costs rose, the deficit was cut through spending cuts and tax rises, banks with property losses needed new capital, and households and firms kept paying down debt. All of these reduced spending at the same time.

Why did Spain's borrowing costs jump?

The 10-year spread over Germany went from 9 bp on average in 2007 to 555 bp in Jul 2012 (monthly average).

Before 2008 markets treated all euro government debt almost the same. Greece's rescue showed that a euro country might not repay, and investors started to price that risk. In Spain the fear centred on the banks: if property losses forced the state to rescue them, public debt would jump, and the banks themselves held much of that public debt. Spain's 10-year yield reached 6.79% in Jul 2012 (Germany 1.24%). After July 2012 the risk of a break-up of the euro faded, and by December 2013 the spread was 234 bp.

2010–2013 · 02

Why did the economy shrink again in 2012–2013?

Spain's GDP fell 2.9% in 2012 and 1.4% in 2013; the euro area's −0.9% and −0.1%.

How to read it. Yearly change in real GDP, %. Bars below zero are years in which output shrank.

Three forces pulled spending down together. Fiscal consolidation: the deficit went from 11.2% of GDP in 2009 to 7.5% in 2013 (2012, at 11.5%, includes the one-off cost of recapitalising banks), aiming to keep investors willing to lend. Deleveraging: households and firms used their income to repay debt instead of spending. Scarce credit: while the spread was high, banks paid more for funding and lent less. With all three pulling in the same direction, domestic demand fell for a second time.

2010–2013 · 03

Where did the debt go?

Household and company debt fell from 234% of GDP (Q2 2010) to 152% in 2019; public debt rose from 36% (2007) to 104% (2014).

How to read it. Both lines are Spanish debt as a % of GDP. Private = households and non-financial companies (BIS, quarterly). Public = general government debt (Eurostat, yearly).

Private debt came down through repayments, defaults and write-offs. Public debt went up through deficits, support for banks and a shrinking GDP (the same debt is a larger share of a smaller economy). The debt did not disappear: part of it moved from private balance sheets to the state's. After a credit boom this is a common pattern.

2010–2013 · 04

How deep did the labour market fall?

Unemployment peaked at 26.4% (Feb 2013), and at 56.2% for under-25s (Jul 2013).

How to read it. Unemployment rate for all ages and for people under 25, % of the labour force.

At the peak more than one in four people looking for work had none (euro area 12.3%). Long spells without work erode skills and make it harder to find a job later, a cost that lasts after the recession ends. House prices kept falling until Q4 2013: in real terms they ended 45% below the 2007 peak, adding losses for banks and for households.

Period 4 of 6 · 2014–2019

What drove the recovery?

Growth averaged +2.6% a year (+2.0% in the euro area) and unemployment fell from 25.5% to 13.8%.

Lower borrowing costs after 2012, ECB rates near zero, cheaper oil from 2014 and a gain in cost competitiveness all helped. The biggest change was in the external accounts: Spain started to sell more abroad than it bought.

Part of the turnaround came from weaker imports while home demand was low; part came from firms that started to export when the home market shrank. Spain's export share is still below the euro area's (47% in 2019), partly because large countries trade less relative to their size. A surplus means Spain lends to the rest of the world instead of borrowing from it, which reduces the dependence on foreign funding that made 2010–2012 so dangerous.

2014–2019 · 02

Did costs come back in line with the euro area?

Spain's unit labour costs fell 7% from 2009 to 2016, while Germany's rose 10%.

How to read it. Unit labour costs, 1999 = 100: the wage cost of producing one unit of output. A rising line means each unit costs more to make.

Inside the euro a country cannot make its currency cheaper. It can only regain price competitiveness by letting costs grow more slowly than its partners: an internal devaluation. Part of Spain's improvement came from the loss of low-productivity jobs, mainly in construction. Output per worker rises when the least productive jobs disappear, which is not the same as each worker becoming more productive.

2014–2019 · 03

What was still fragile?

In 2019 unemployment was 13.8% (euro area 7.5%) and public debt 98% of GDP (euro area 84%).

How to read it. General government debt, % of GDP, for Spain, the euro area and Germany.

Public debt stopped rising but barely came down, so Spain entered the next crisis with debt near 98% of GDP, against 36% in 2007. 26% of employees were again on temporary contracts (16% in the euro area), so jobs could still be cut quickly in a downturn. Output per hour worked was +13.3% above 2007 (euro area +9.3%).

Period 5 of 6 · 2020–2022

Why did Covid hit Spain harder, and what happened to inflation?

Real GDP fell 10.9% in 2020 (euro area 6.0%); inflation peaked at 10.7% in Jul 2022.

Lockdowns stopped tourism, hospitality and leisure, which are a larger part of Spain's economy than of most of its neighbours. When economies reopened, energy prices jumped, first with the reopening and then with the war in Ukraine, and in 2022 the ECB raised rates for the first time in eleven years.

How high did inflation go, and why did it turn earlier in Spain?

Spain peaked at 10.7% in Jul 2022, the euro area at 10.6% in Oct 2022.

How to read it. Yearly change in consumer prices (HICP), %. Top: all items. Bottom: energy only (electricity, gas, fuels). Dashed line: Russia's invasion of Ukraine (Feb 2022), which pushed gas prices up.

Energy explains most of the timing. Spanish energy inflation peaked at 60% in Mar 2022, the euro area's at 44% in Mar 2022. Many Spanish households had electricity tariffs linked to the daily wholesale price, so higher gas prices reached their bills within weeks, and so did the later fall. From June 2022 Spain and Portugal also capped the gas price used to set electricity prices (the "Iberian exception"). In most of the euro area fixed-price contracts passed the rise on later. From Sep 2022 Spanish inflation was below the euro area's. Energy prices were already falling on the year in Spain by Dec 2022, and in the euro area by Mar 2023.

2020–2022 · 03

How fast did interest rates rise?

The ECB deposit rate went from −0.50% to 4.00% between Jul 2022 and Sep 2023.

How to read it. All in %. The ECB deposit rate is what banks earn on money left at the ECB, the rate that steers the others. The 3-month rate is Euribor. The Spanish 10-year yield is what the state pays to borrow for ten years.

Most Spanish mortgages have variable rates reset to Euribor every 6 or 12 months, so rate rises passed quickly into household budgets; where fixed-rate mortgages dominate the effect is slower. The 3-month rate peaked at 3.97% and the state's 10-year yield went from 0.41% (Dec 2021) to 3.95% (Oct 2023). With public debt at 109% of GDP in 2022, higher yields raise the state's interest bill over time as old debt is refinanced.

Key ideas

What do the terms on this page mean?

Twelve ideas used across the tabs, in plain language.

Real vs nominal
Nominal figures are in today's euros. Real figures remove inflation, so they measure quantities (how much was produced) rather than prices.
Real interest rate
The interest rate minus inflation. If a loan costs 3% and prices rise 4%, the real rate is −1%: the debt shrinks in real terms, so borrowing is very attractive.
Current account
What a country earns from the rest of the world (exports, tourism, income) minus what it pays. A deficit has to be financed by borrowing from abroad or selling assets to foreigners.
Unit labour cost
Wages paid per unit of output. It rises when wages grow faster than productivity, which makes a country's goods more expensive compared with its trading partners.
Spread and basis point
The spread is the extra yield Spain pays over Germany on 10-year bonds. One basis point (bp) is 0.01 percentage point, so 100 bp = 1 percentage point.
Internal devaluation
Inside the euro a country cannot make its currency cheaper. It can only regain price competitiveness by letting wages and prices grow more slowly than its partners.
Deleveraging
Households, firms or banks reducing their debt by repaying it, which means spending less than their income for a while.
Automatic stabilisers
Taxes fall and unemployment benefits rise by themselves in a recession, so the budget deficit grows without any new decision.
Temporary contract
A fixed-term job contract. It is cheaper to end than a permanent one, so in a downturn firms cut these jobs first.
PPS (purchasing power standard)
An artificial currency that removes price-level differences between countries, so GDP per person can be compared fairly. EU average = 100.
HICP
The harmonised index of consumer prices: the inflation measure used by Eurostat and the ECB, built the same way in every EU country.
Percent vs percentage point
Unemployment going from 10% to 12% is a rise of 2 percentage points, or 20%.

Summary

What did we learn?

Seven takeaways, ending with the limits.

  1. One interest rate, different inflation. Inside the euro Spain's real short-term rate averaged 0.1% in 1999–2007. Cheap real credit took household and company debt from 85% to 216% of GDP and real house prices up 126%.
  2. The imbalances of the boom were private and external. Public debt was 36% of GDP in 2007, but the current account deficit reached 9.9% of GDP and unit labour costs rose 35% against +3% in Germany.
  3. Spain's labour market amplifies shocks. Unemployment went from 7.9% to 26.4%, with construction behind 65% of the jobs lost in 2007–2009 and 32% of employees on temporary contracts.
  4. Without its own currency, the adjustment came through costs and exports. Unit labour costs fell 7% from 2009 to 2016, exports rose from 23% to 35% of GDP, and the current account has been in surplus since 2012.
  5. Structure decides how a shock spreads. A large tourism sector made the Covid fall deeper (−22.0% in Q2 2020), and electricity prices linked to the wholesale market made inflation rise and fall earlier.
  6. Recent growth comes mostly from more workers. Since 2023 employment grew +2.9% a year and output per worker 0.0%. GDP per person is 91% of the EU average and unemployment 10.3% (euro area 6.5%).
  7. Limits. Period averages hide what happens inside each period, and recent years will be revised. The euro-area figures are for today's 20 members, calculated back in time. The mechanisms on this page are the standard economic explanations; the charts show that the numbers moved together, not proof of what caused what. The page describes what happened and does not judge the policies chosen.
