How does Black-Scholes turn five numbers into a price?

Measure the distance to the strike in typical moves, then read probabilities off the bell curve.

How to read it. Each line is one step of the formula, with today's number on the right. σ√T = 0.0501 is the typical move over 30 days: 5.0%, or about 382 index points. d1 and d2 say how many of those moves today's level sits above the strike; N() turns them into probabilities.

σ√T0.0501
d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)·T] / σ√T0.0950
d2 = d1 − σ√T0.0449
N(d1), N(d2)0.5378, 0.5179
Call = S·N(d1) − K·e^(−rT)·N(d2)$165.89
Put = K·e^(−rT)·N(−d2) − S·N(−d1)$139.21
Call option
The right, not the obligation, to buy the index at the strike on the expiry date. Worth something only if the index ends above the strike.
Put option
The right to sell at the strike. Worth something only if the index ends below the strike: it works like insurance against a fall.
Strike (K) and expiry (T)
The agreed price and the date. Here K is today's level rounded to the nearest 5 ("at the money") and T is 30 days, written in years.
Volatility (σ) and the VIX
How much the index typically moves in a year, in %. The VIX is the market's own estimate for the next 30 days, worked out from option prices.
Risk-free rate (r)
The interest on a safe 3-month US Treasury bill. Money due at expiry is worth a little less today: K × e^(−rT).
N(x), the bell curve
The share of a normal (bell-shaped) distribution to the left of x: a probability between 0 and 1. N(0) = 0.5.
Delta
How much the option price moves when the index moves 1 point. A call delta of 0.5 means about 50 cents per point.
Bid, ask and mid
The best prices buyers offer (bid) and sellers ask (ask). The mid, halfway between, is a fair estimate of the market price.

The call is what you expect to receive, S·N(d1), minus what you expect to pay, the strike's value today times the chance of paying it, N(d2) = 0.52. With the index right at the strike these chances sit near one half, so the call and the put land close to each other; the gap that remains is mostly interest (panels 3 and 4).

04

Do the call and the put agree with each other?

Yes: call − put = 26.6781 and S − K·e−rT = 26.6781.

How to read it. Put-call parity. Buying a call and selling a put with the same strike and expiry pays exactly (index − strike) at expiry, whatever happens: the same as owning the index and owing the strike. Two positions that always pay the same must cost the same today, so call − put has to equal S − K·e−rT. No volatility is involved.

The two sides match to 9 decimal places: any gap left is only the computer's rounding, so the call and put formulas are consistent with each other. If real prices broke this rule, a trader could buy the cheap side, sell the dear side and lock in a riskless profit, which is why markets keep it close (apart from dividends, which this simple version leaves out).

The model is too expensive, and the volatility input is the main suspect. The VIX is not the volatility of this one option: it is an average over many strikes, and it leans on puts far below today's level, which are expensive because investors pay up for crash protection. So the VIX usually sits above the volatility traders price into an at-the-money option. A lower σ would bring the model down to the market.

Leaving out dividends pushes the same way: the companies in the index pay dividends, which lower the expected future level of the index, and so make calls cheaper and puts dearer than this simple version says.

06

What did we learn?

Takeaways, then the limits.

  1. Five numbers set the price. S&P 500 7,625.87, strike 7,625, rate 0.04125, volatility 0.1748 and 30 days give a call of $165.89 and a put of $139.21: about 2.2% and 1.8% of the index for a month of protection.
  2. Delta is the slope of the price line. The call moves about 0.54 and the put −0.46 for each point the index moves. The two always differ by exactly 1.
  3. Parity holds to 9 decimal places. Call − put equals S − K·e−rT, as it must for any pricing that leaves no free money on the table. It is a check on the formulas, not on the market.
  4. The market disagrees. For the closest real call the model is $28.29 (21.0%) above it. The model is only as good as its volatility input.
  5. Limits. One snapshot on one day, with Yahoo's quotes, which can be delayed by about 15 minutes. Black-Scholes assumes volatility and interest rates stay constant and that the index never jumps; real markets break all three. The VIX is used as a proxy for this option's volatility, dividends are left out, and the T-bill rate is used as quoted, without converting it to continuous compounding.
